Part 2 — The Automation Script

This is the exact sequence from the previous chapter, laid out as steps. Do this in one sitting — it takes under ten minutes.

  1. Check which option you have. Log into your employer's payroll portal or ask HR: "Can I split my direct deposit between two accounts, or send a fixed dollar amount to savings?" If yes, go to step 2. If no, skip to step 4.
  2. If split deposit is available: open the direct deposit form, add your savings account's routing and account number, and set it to a flat dollar amount (not a percentage) matching what you wrote in Part 1.
  3. Confirm the split. Most portals show a preview before your next pay run — check that checking still gets the remainder and savings gets your target amount. Save/submit.
  4. If split deposit isn't available: open your bank's app, go to "Transfers" or "Recurring Transfers," and set up an automatic transfer from checking to savings.
  5. Set the transfer date to your payday, not a few days after. The RCT didn't test this exact timing, but the same logic applies: money you've already moved isn't money you can talk yourself out of.
  6. Set the amount to what you wrote in Part 1.
  7. Name the savings account something that isn't neutral — "Buffer," "$500 Fund," "Don't Touch." A one-word label does real work at 11 p.m. when you're tempted to move it back.
  8. Screenshot or write down the confirmation. This is your proof the automation exists — check it in 30 days, not your willpower.

If you can't do split deposit or an automatic transfer today for any reason, the fallback is a recurring calendar reminder with the same dollar amount and the same day. Be honest with yourself about what it is: even the study's "active choice" arm — a one-time forced yes-or-no decision, which is a stronger push than any passive reminder — moved participation no more than 5 points, versus the 40-plus-point jumps true automation produced. A reminder is weaker still. But it beats doing nothing while you sort out the automated option.

Part 3 — The Stop-and-Redirect Rule

The day your balance hits $500, don't let the automation quietly keep going into a fund that's already served its purpose without a plan for what's next.

When you hit $500, do one of these two things — same day:

  1. If you have any debt with an interest rate above ~10% (most credit cards, some personal loans): redirect the automated transfer to extra debt payments instead of savings. Keep the $500 buffer intact and untouched — it's still doing its job — but stop growing it further until high-interest debt is handled. You're not removing the buffer; you're pointing the automation at the next problem.
  2. If you have no high-interest debt, or once it's cleared: keep the exact same automation running, aimed at the next tier — $750, then working toward $2,000. You don't need a new decision or a new system. Same transfer, same day, same amount (or raise it by $5-10 per paycheck once $500 is behind you).

Either way, the mechanism doesn't change. You're not starting over. You're redirecting a system that's already proven it works.
